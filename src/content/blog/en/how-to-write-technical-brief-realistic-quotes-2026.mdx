## The Problem: Quotes That Skyrocket

An email arrives from your development provider: *"The project is going to cost double the estimate. We need 3 more months."*

If you've experienced this, the problem probably wasn't the provider. It was the **technical brief**.

A poorly written brief is like asking an architect to build you a house by saying *"I want something nice with 3 bedrooms"*. The result will be unpredictable, expensive, and probably not what you need.

In 2026, with development costs rising 8% annually, a solid technical brief isn't a luxury. It's insurance against overruns, delays, and broken vendor relationships.

This article gives you a practical framework for writing briefs that get realistic quotes from day one.

---

## What Is a Technical Brief (And What It Isn't)

A technical brief is **not**:
- A feature list copied from your competitor
- A 2-page document with general ideas
- An email saying *"I want something like Uber but for X"*

A technical brief **is**:
- A document describing the business problem, users, features, integrations, and constraints
- The contractual foundation of your relationship with the provider
- The tool that aligns expectations before the clock starts ticking

**Golden rule:** If a provider gives you a quote in 24 hours without asking questions, they're either selling you smoke or the price will be a lottery ticket.

## The 6 Sections Every Technical Brief Must Have

### 1. Context and Business Problem

**What most people write:** *"I want an app to manage orders."*

**What you should write:**
- What current process are you trying to improve or replace?
- How much does that process cost you today (in time, money, errors)?
- What happens if you do nothing?
- Who makes the final decision and who will use the system?

**Real example:**
> "We currently manage orders via WhatsApp and Excel. One employee spends 4 hours daily transcribing messages into spreadsheets. We average 8 errors per week that cost €200 each in returns. We need to reduce those errors to less than 1 per week and free up those 4 daily hours for customer service."

This paragraph tells the provider: the problem costs €1,600/week + €1,600/week in errors = **€3,200/week**. Now they know the budget must be justified against that opportunity cost.

---

### 2. Users and Main Flows

Define **who** will use the system and **what they'll do** with it.

**Users:**
- Role (administrator, customer, employee, supplier...)
- Technical level (are they tech-savvy or do they need something ultra-intuitive?)
- Primary device (mobile, desktop, tablet)
- Usage frequency (daily, weekly, occasional)

**Main flows (User Stories):**
> "As a [role], I want [action], so that [benefit]"

**Example:**
> "As a delivery driver, I want to see my optimized daily route on mobile, so I can deliver all orders in the shortest time possible without depending on the office."

**How many flows to include:** Between 5 and 15 main flows cover 80% of the value for most projects. More than 20 and you're describing a complete product, not an MVP.

---

### 3. Features: Must-Have vs Nice-to-Have

The most ignored rule in technical briefs: **not everything is a priority**.

Divide into three categories:

| Priority | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Must-have (P0)** | Without this, the project makes no sense | Drivers can see their routes |
| **Should-have (P1)** | Important, but can be delayed 1-2 months | Real-time push notifications |
| **Nice-to-have (P2)** | Would improve experience, but not critical | Dark mode, Instagram integration |

**Why this matters for the quote:**
A provider seeing 20 P0 features knows you're asking for a complete product. One seeing 5 P0 and 15 P1/P2 knows you can launch in phases, reducing initial cost by 40-60%.

---

### 4. Integrations and External Dependencies

This section separates realistic quotes from fantasy ones.

**Key questions:**
- Does it need to connect with existing systems? (ERP, CRM, payment gateway...)
- Are there documented APIs or is web scraping needed?
- Does it require authentication with Google, Microsoft, SAML?
- Does it need to comply with specific regulations? (GDPR, healthcare, legal...)

**Example of an integration that doubles the budget if not mentioned:**
> "We need the app to integrate with our SAP Business One. It doesn't have a REST API, only SQL Server database access."

A provider reading this knows they'll need:
- Direct database access (security risk)
- Or develop an integration middleware
- Or budget for a third-party integration license

Without this information, the initial quote will be fictional and the surprise will hit in week 3.

---

### 5. Technical and Business Constraints

**Technical:**
- Preferred (or prohibited) tech stack
- Supported browsers/devices
- Performance requirements ("page must load in `<2` seconds")
- Expected scalability limits ("prepared for 10,000 users in 6 months")

**Business:**
- Launch deadline (is there an event, campaign, or regulation?)
- Approximate maximum budget (yes, sharing it helps adjust scope)
- Available internal team (do you have a CTO who will review code or do you need full management?)

**About sharing your budget:**
Many clients hide their budget thinking the provider will max it out. The reality: a serious provider will tell you *"with €15,000 we can do A, B, and C; with €30,000 we add D and E"*. Hiding the budget only generates miscalibrated proposals.

---

### 6. Acceptance Criteria and Definition of "Done"

**The most important question:** How will we know the project is ready?

Define:
- Tests it must pass (functional, performance, security)
- Deliverable documentation (user manual, technical documentation, architecture diagrams)
- Training for internal team
- Post-launch warranty period

**Example:**
> "The project is considered complete when:
> - All P0 flows pass automated tests
> - API documentation is delivered for future integrations
> - 3 test users use the system for 1 week without reporting critical bugs
> - A 2-hour training session is held for the admin team"

This eliminates discussions like *"but I thought it included..."*.

## Quick Template: Checklist for Your Next Brief

Before sending your brief to a provider, verify:

- [ ] I've described the business problem with numbers (current cost, cost of inaction)
- [ ] I've defined the main users and their flows (minimum 5, maximum 15)
- [ ] I've classified features into Must-have / Should-have / Nice-to-have
- [ ] I've listed all integrations with existing systems
- [ ] I've mentioned technical constraints (stack, devices, performance)
- [ ] I've indicated the deadline and approximate budget
- [ ] I've defined what "project complete" means
- [ ] I've attached mockups, wireframes, or visual references (even hand-drawn sketches)

If you check fewer than 6 of these 8 boxes, your brief needs more work before requesting quotes.

## Common Mistakes That Inflate Quotes

### Mistake 1: "I Want Everything Just Like My Competitor"

Your competitor has 5 years and €500,000 invested. You're just starting. A provider receiving this request will either quote €500,000 (and lose the project) or give an unrealistic price to win the work (and then there will be overruns).

**Solution:** Define what YOU need, not what your competitor has.

### Mistake 2: Not Mentioning the Current State

If you have a 2018 WordPress site and want "something modern", the provider needs to know:
- Are we migrating data or starting from scratch?
- Is there content to preserve?
- Are there existing users with passwords they can't lose?

Migrating 5,000 users with purchase history is not the same as launching from zero.

### Mistake 3: Asking for "Something Flexible That Scales"

Everyone wants something that scales. But to what scale?

- 100 users → Any modern stack works
- 10,000 users → You need architecture decisions
- 1,000,000 users → You need a dedicated infrastructure team

**Solution:** Define the scenario at 6, 12, and 24 months. Don't ask for "scalable" without numbers.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring Post-Launch Maintenance

Development is 40% of a project's total cost over 5 years. Maintenance is 60%.

A brief that doesn't mention:
- Hosting and domain
- Security updates
- Technical support
- Product evolution

...is asking for a partial quote on something that will cost double.

## How to Review a Quote Based on Your Brief

When you receive proposals, verify that the provider has addressed:

1. **Have they included all P0 features?** If any are missing, the quote is incomplete
2. **Have they broken it down by phases?** A €50,000 proposal without phases is a red flag
3. **Do they mention technical risks?** A provider who sees no risks hasn't read the brief carefully
4. **Do they include testing and warranty?** 30% of development time should be testing
5. **Is there a communication plan?** Weekly meetings? Bi-weekly demos? Access to a testing environment?

**Warning sign:** A quote that's 40% cheaper than the others without a clear explanation. They're probably underestimating complexity or outsourcing to low-cost providers without quality.

## Conclusion: A Good Brief Is a Good Contract

A well-written technical brief doesn't guarantee a perfect project. But it does guarantee that:
- You and the provider start from the same foundation
- The quote reflects the real scope
- There are fewer surprises along the way
- The business relationship starts with transparency

**Invest 4-8 hours in writing a good brief.** It will save you weeks of misunderstandings and thousands of euros in scope changes.
