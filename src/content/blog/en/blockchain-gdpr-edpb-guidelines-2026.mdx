## Introduction

On **July 9, 2026**, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) adopted the long-awaited **Guidelines on the processing of personal data through blockchain technologies**. This document, in which the Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) was the main rapporteur, resolves one of the most complex tensions in European digital law: **how to reconcile the inherent immutability of blockchain with citizens' rights** recognized under the GDPR.

The prior public consultation broke participation records, becoming the EDPB text that has received the most contributions to date. This demonstrates the enormous interest —and urgency— of having a clear framework for a technology already present in sensitive sectors such as financial, healthcare, and increasingly, the **legal sector**.

In this article, we analyze the guidelines point by point and provide a **practical implementation guide** for law firms and businesses operating in the Spanish and European environment.

---

## 1. The Conflict: Immutability vs. Digital Rights

Blockchain functions as a **digitally replicated ledger** across multiple locations, where data is cryptographically linked. Once information is validated, it cannot be easily altered or deleted. This immutability guarantees system integrity but directly clashes with GDPR principles such as:

- **Storage limitation** (Art. 5.1.e)
- **Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten")** (Art. 17)
- **Accuracy** (Art. 5.1.d)
- **Data minimization** (Art. 5.1.c)

Until now, many blockchain projects operated in a **regulatory gray zone**. The new EDPB guidelines eliminate that uncertainty and establish a clear framework: **blockchain technology is compatible with the GDPR, but only if designed from the outset under a transparent governance model oriented toward privacy**.

## 2. The Four Pillars of the EDPB Guidelines

### 2.1. Avoid Plain-Text Personal Data on the Chain

The EDPB **explicitly discourages** storing personal data directly within the block structure. Instead, it recommends a hybrid architecture:

| Component | What it stores | Where |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| **Off-chain** | Identifiable personal data | Company's conventional systems |
| **On-chain** | Cryptographic proof of existence | Blockchain (hash, cryptographic commitments) |

**Practical example:**

A digital notary certifying a contract signature should not store the signer's name, ID number, or contract content on the blockchain. Instead, it registers a **cryptographic hash** that proves the document existed at a specific date and time. If someone later needs to verify authenticity, they compare the original document's hash with the one recorded on the chain.

```python
# Conceptual example of on-chain / off-chain separation
import hashlib

def register_blockchain_proof(document: str) -> str:
    """
    Generates a hash of the document and registers it on blockchain.
    The original document is NEVER stored on the chain.
    """
    document_hash = hashlib.sha256(document.encode()).hexdigest()
    # register_hash_on_blockchain(document_hash)
    return document_hash
```

### 2.2. Privacy by Design

The guidelines establish that **data protection cannot be improvised**. Organizations must incorporate advanced technical minimization and encryption measures from the initial conception phase of the project.

**Privacy by Design Checklist for Blockchain Projects:**

- [ ] Has an impact assessment (DPIA) been conducted before development?
- [ ] Is personal data stored off-chain by default?
- [ ] Is end-to-end encryption used for off-chain data?
- [ ] Is there a key rotation mechanism?
- [ ] Has a maximum retention period been defined for off-chain data?
- [ ] Does the smart contract include access revocation functions?

### 2.3. Guarantee of Citizens' Rights

The EDPB clarifies that rights of access, rectification, objection, and erasure are **technologically neutral** and must be fully guaranteed, regardless of the underlying technology.

To make the right to erasure effective in an immutable environment, the guidelines propose **technical formulas**:

| Right | Proposed Technical Solution |
|-------|----------------------------|
| **Erasure** | Destruction of off-chain data decryption keys |
| **Rectification** | Update off-chain record + new hash on chain |
| **Objection** | Revocation of permissions in the smart contract |
| **Access** | Query off-chain record via authorized key |

**Key concept:** Destroying the decryption keys makes a blockchain record **effectively and irreversibly anonymized** from third parties' perspective, even though the hash remains on the chain.

### 2.4. Mandatory Impact Assessments (DPIA)

Due to the technical risks inherent in massive data replication and international transfers associated with distributed network nodes, the EDPB establishes that **entities must conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** before launching any processing with this technology.

**Elements the DPIA for blockchain must include:**

1. **Node map:** geographic location of all nodes that will replicate data
2. **International transfer analysis:** if any node is outside the EEA
3. **Governance model:** who controls the network, how decisions are made
4. **Revocation mechanisms:** how ARCO+ rights are exercised
5. **Contingency plan:** what happens if a compromised node leaves the network

## 3. Practical Implications for Law Firms

### 3.1. Advising Clients with Blockchain Projects

Law firms advising fintech startups, DeFi projects, or companies with blockchain traceability must update their **compliance memoranda** to include:

- Review of on-chain vs. off-chain architecture
- Verification that no personal data exists in plain text on the chain
- Smart contract review to guarantee ARCO+ rights
- DPIA recommendation before launch

### 3.2. Internal Use of Blockchain in the Firm

Some firms are experimenting with blockchain for:

- **Document certification:** timestamps for contracts and deeds
- **Digital chain of custody:** traceability of electronic evidence
- **Communication notarization:** proof of sending and receiving digital communications

**Recommendation:** Before implementing any internal blockchain solution, conduct a DPIA and ensure the architecture complies with the EDPB's four pillars.

### 3.3. Business Opportunity

The new guidelines generate demand for specialized legal services:

- **Blockchain-GDPR compliance audits**
- **Drafting of DPAs (Data Processing Agreements) for distributed networks**
- **Regulatory architecture design** for blockchain projects
- **Internal training** for legal and technical teams

## 4. Practical Implementation Cases

### Case A: Notarial Certification with Blockchain

**Situation:** A notary wants to certify the existence of public deeds using blockchain.

**EDPB-Compliant Solution:**

1. The deed is stored in the notary's document system (off-chain)
2. A SHA-256 hash of the deed is generated
3. The hash is registered on the blockchain with a timestamp
4. The notary retains the decryption keys
5. If the holder exercises the right to erasure, the off-chain keys are destroyed

**Result:** The proof of existence remains on the chain, but personal data becomes irreversibly inaccessible.

### Case B: Product Traceability (Supply Chain)

**Situation:** A food company wants to trace the origin of its products with blockchain.

**EDPB-Compliant Solution:**

1. Farmer data (name, ID, farm) is stored in a private database
2. Only the following is registered on blockchain: `product_hash + origin_certificate_hash`
3. The end consumer scans a QR code that queries the off-chain database
4. If the farmer requests erasure, their off-chain data is deleted and the link is revoked

## 5. Relationship with Other European Regulations

The EDPB guidelines do not operate in a vacuum. Blockchain projects must also consider:

| Regulation | Implication for Blockchain |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| **AI Act** | If blockchain interacts with AI systems (e.g., automated validation) |
| **DORA** | For financial entities using blockchain in financial services |
| **NIS2** | Critical node operators may be important entities |
| **eIDAS 2.0** | The EUDI Wallet may integrate with blockchain identities |
| **Data Act** | Sharing IoT data registered on blockchain |

## 6. Compliance Checklist for Blockchain Projects

### Design Phase

- [ ] DPIA conducted and documented
- [ ] On-chain/off-chain architecture defined
- [ ] Personal data identified and classified
- [ ] Encryption and key management mechanism established
- [ ] Retention periods defined for off-chain data

### Development Phase

- [ ] Smart contract audited (security + privacy)
- [ ] Access revocation functions implemented
- [ ] Audit logs activated
- [ ] Penetration testing conducted

### Operation Phase

- [ ] Procedure for responding to ARCO+ rights exercises
- [ ] Service Level Agreements (SLA) with network nodes
- [ ] Quarterly compliance audits
- [ ] Team training in data governance

## Conclusion

The EDPB guidelines of July 2026 represent a **turning point** for blockchain adoption in Europe. Far from prohibiting the technology, the regulator has opted for a **pragmatic and proportionate approach**: blockchain is compatible with the GDPR if correctly designed from the start.

For law firms, this represents both an **update obligation** (clients will ask about this) and a **business opportunity** (few firms master the intersection of blockchain, GDPR, and contract law).

**The key is in the architecture:** separate what goes on-chain (cryptographic proofs) from what goes off-chain (personal data), design for privacy from day one, and document everything through a rigorous DPIA.
