## The bill nobody expects

You've invested €30,000 in developing your application. The project is finished, users are using it, and you breathe a sigh of relief thinking the worst is over.

Six months later, your developer sends you a bill for €2,500. "Monthly maintenance," it says. And that's not the only expense: the server costs €400, there's an urgent security update requiring €1,800, and a bug affecting 15 users needs immediate attention.

**Welcome to the hidden cost of software.**

In the industry, there's an unwritten rule: software maintenance costs between 40% and 70% of the initial development budget... every year. It's not an anomaly. It's the norm. And most companies don't plan for it because nobody explains it before they sign the contract.

In this article, we're going to dissect software maintenance: what it includes, what it really costs, why it's inevitable, and how to plan for it so it doesn't break your budget.

---

## What is software maintenance, really?

It's not just "fixing bugs when they appear." Maintenance covers four distinct categories, each with its own rhythm and cost:

### 1. Corrective maintenance (the one everyone hates)

**What it is:** Fixing bugs, errors, and unexpected behaviors that appear in production.

**When it happens:** Always. There is no software without bugs. The more complex the system, the more edge cases appear when real users start using it in ways you didn't anticipate.

**What it costs:** Depends on severity. A visual bug might cost €200. A bug that corrupts data can cost €5,000+ and require service downtime.

**Real example:** One of our clients had a bug in their booking system that allowed double bookings in the same time slot. It appeared after 8 months of use. Fix cost: €1,200. Cost of double bookings and complaints: €3,400.

### 2. Adaptive maintenance (the one nobody anticipates)

**What it is:** Modifying software so it works in a changing environment. Third-party APIs update versions. Browsers change standards. Operating systems stop supporting libraries.

**When it happens:** Constantly. The technology ecosystem doesn't stop. What worked in January might break in March because Stripe updated their API, or because Chrome changed how it handles certain elements.

**What it costs:** €500-€3,000 per major update, depending on how many external systems you use.

**Real example:** In 2025, Google changed its third-party cookie policy. Every company with Google Analytics integration, Google login, or advertising had to update their code. For a medium-sized app, that meant €2,000-€5,000 in adaptive development.

### 3. Perfective maintenance (the one users demand)

**What it is:** Improvements and new features that weren't in the original MVP but that users need to keep using the product.

**When it happens:** From day one. Users always ask for more: "Could I export to Excel?", "What if it had push notifications?", "Can I integrate it with Slack?"

**What it costs:** Variable. But this is where most money goes. Successful companies invest 60% of their product budget in continuous improvements, not in maintaining what exists.

**The uncomfortable truth:** If you don't improve your product, it stagnates. And a stagnant product is a dead product.

### 4. Preventive maintenance (the one everyone ignores until it's too late)

**What it is:** Security updates, patches, performance optimization, and code refactoring before it becomes a problem.

**When it happens:** Should be continuous. In practice, only done when something explodes.

**What it costs:** €300-€1,000 monthly if done right. €10,000+ if ignored and then there's a security breach or the system collapses.

**Real example:** A law firm ignored security updates for their client portal for 18 months. Result: exploited vulnerability, data from 200 clients compromised, €15,000 in incident response, and incalculable reputational damage.

## The math of maintenance: Real numbers

Let's be honest with the numbers. Suppose a medium-complexity web application, developed for €40,000:

| Item | Frequency | Estimated annual cost |
|---|---|---|
| Hosting and servers | Monthly | €3,600 - €6,000 |
| Bugs and fixes | Ongoing | €4,000 - €8,000 |
| Security updates | Quarterly | €1,200 - €3,000 |
| API and dependency updates | Semi-annual | €2,000 - €5,000 |
| Improvements and new features | Ongoing | €8,000 - €20,000 |
| Monitoring and alerts | Monthly | €600 - €1,200 |
| Backups and disaster recovery | Monthly | €400 - €800 |
| **TOTAL ANNUAL** | | **€19,800 - €44,000** |

**Annual maintenance costs between 50% and 110% of initial development.**

And this is for a stable application. If your software is business-critical, if it has many users, or if it depends on multiple external integrations, it goes up.

## The mistakes that multiply maintenance costs

### Mistake 1: "We're done, we don't need more development"

Software isn't furniture you buy once. It's a garden that requires pruning, watering, and replanting. Ignoring maintenance doesn't save money. It accumulates technical debt that later collects interest.

### Mistake 2: Hiring the cheapest developer for maintenance

Maintenance requires understanding existing code. A cheap developer who doesn't know the system can take 3x longer to fix something than someone familiar with it. And they can break other things in the process.

### Mistake 3: Not having a maintenance budget from the start

Many companies plan for development but not maintenance. Result: they run out of funds for critical bug fixes, or accumulate technical debt that later costs 5x more to resolve.

### Mistake 4: Accumulating technical debt to "go faster"

"We'll do it quickly now and fix it later." That "later" never comes. And every shortcut you take today is a bug, a refactoring, or a limitation that will cost you double tomorrow.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring security updates

Vulnerabilities aren't theoretical. In 2025, 43% of security breaches in SMEs were due to outdated software. Average cost of a breach: €25,000 for small companies, €200,000+ for medium ones.

## How to plan maintenance without surprises

### 1. Budget for maintenance from day 1

Practical rule: reserve 50-70% of development cost annually for maintenance. If your app cost €40,000, budget €20,000-€28,000 per year.

It's not an extra expense. It's part of the total cost of software ownership.

### 2. Separate maintenance from new features

Create two distinct budgets:
- **Maintenance:** bugs, security, updates, hosting. This is non-negotiable.
- **Improvements:** new features. This is flexible and prioritizable.

If you need to cut, cut improvements. Never critical maintenance.

### 3. Establish a monthly maintenance contract

Instead of paying by the hour every time something breaks, negotiate a monthly maintenance contract that includes:
- X hours of corrective development
- Security updates
- Basic monitoring
- Guaranteed response time (SLA)

**Advantage:** Predictable cost. Clear prioritization. You're not negotiating prices while your system is down.

### 4. Automate what you can

- CI/CD for automatic deployments
- Automated tests to detect regressions
- Dependabot or similar for automatic security updates
- Monitoring with proactive alerts

Every hour invested in automation saves 10 hours of manual maintenance.

### 5. Document everything (yes, everything)

Good documentation reduces maintenance costs by 30-40%:
- System architecture
- Important technical decisions
- How to deploy
- How to debug common problems
- List of dependencies and versions

**Initial investment:** €500-€1,500. **Annual savings:** €3,000-€6,000 in comprehension time.

## When does maintenance stop being worth it?

There's a point where maintaining costs more than rebuilding. Signs you've reached that point:

- 70% of development time goes to fixing things, not improving
- Dependency updates break more than they fix
- The technology stack is 8+ years old and there are no developers who know it
- Annual maintenance cost exceeds 80% of rebuild cost

In that case, it's not maintenance. It's life support. And sometimes, the most economical choice is to start from scratch.

## Real case: From €60,000 annually to €24,000

One of our clients, an e-learning platform with 5,000 users, was spending €60,000 annually on chaotic maintenance:
- €24,000 on overprovisioned hosting
- €18,000 on reactive development (bugs, emergencies)
- €12,000 on accumulated security updates
- €6,000 on poorly configured monitoring and backups

**Diagnosis:** Massive technical debt, inefficient infrastructure, zero automation.

**Actions:**
1. Refactoring of critical code (€8,000, one-time)
2. Migration to optimized cloud infrastructure (60% hosting savings)
3. Implementation of CI/CD and automated tests
4. Structured monthly maintenance contract
5. Scheduled quarterly security updates

**Result after 12 months:**
- Hosting: €9,600 (saving €14,400)
- Development: €9,600 (fewer emergencies, more planning)
- Security: €3,600 (continuous updates, not accumulated)
- Monitoring: €1,800 (efficient tools)
- **Total: €24,400** (59% savings)

**The key:** It wasn't about spending less. It was about spending better.

## Conclusion: Maintenance isn't a necessary evil

It's easy to see maintenance as an expense with no return. But the reality is that **software that isn't maintained dies**. And it dies in slow, painful ways: accumulated bugs, frustrated users, competitors with better products, and finally, obsolescence.

Good maintenance isn't just fixing what's broken. It's:
- **Protecting your initial investment**
- **Keeping your product competitive**
- **Preventing crises** that cost 10x more
- **Giving confidence** to your users that the product evolves

> **Software is like a car: it's not just what you pay to buy it, it's what it costs to keep it on the road. And a car without maintenance isn't just slow: it breaks down.**

If you're planning a software project, include maintenance in your budget from the start. If you already have a product running, audit how much you're really spending and where you can optimize.
