## Introduction

The **Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)** of the European Union entered into force in 2026, marking a turning point in the regulation of cybersecurity for digital products. Unlike other regulations that focus on services or infrastructure, the CRA imposes direct obligations on **manufacturers, importers, and distributors of products with digital elements**.

For law firms, the CRA represents both a challenge and an opportunity: their manufacturing clients will need specialized advice to comply with complex technical requirements, and the firms themselves that develop software must assess whether it applies to them.

## What is the Cyber Resilience Act?

### Scope and objectives

The CRA establishes cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements (PDE) marketed in the EU. Its main objective is to ensure that:

- Digital products are designed with security from their conception
- Vulnerabilities are managed proactively throughout the lifecycle
- Users receive clear information about product security
- Cybersecurity incidents are reported in a coordinated manner

### Who does it apply to?

| Category | Obligations |
|----------|-------------|
| **Manufacturers** | Secure design, risk assessment, CE declaration, security manual |
| **Importers** | Verify manufacturer compliance, do not modify products |
| **Distributors** | Verify CE marking, security information available |
| **Firms with proprietary software** | Assess whether their software constitutes a digital product |

### Covered products

The CRA applies to virtually any product with software or connectivity:

- **Connected hardware**: routers, IoT cameras, medical devices
- **Software**: applications, operating systems, firmware
- **Associated digital services**: updates, remote support
- **Consumer products**: wearables, smart appliances

## Product classification by risk

### Class I products (standard risk)

Require compliance with essential requirements and self-declaration:

- Non-critical software
- General mobile applications
- Basic consumer IoT devices

### Class II products (high risk)

Require notification to authority and third-party assessment:

- Operating systems
- Identity management software
- VPN and network products
- Security management systems
- Payment software

### Excluded products

- Non-commercial open-source software
- Pure cloud services (covered by NIS2/DORA)
- Military or defense products
- Medical software already regulated by MDR

## Essential requirements of the CRA

### 1. Security by Design

Products must be designed considering:

```markdown
- [ ] Documented threat and risk analysis
- [ ] Secure architecture with least privilege principle
- [ ] Encryption of sensitive data in transit and at rest
- [ ] Robust authentication and access control
- [ ] Protection against known vulnerabilities (OWASP Top 10)
```

### 2. Vulnerability management

Continuous obligations throughout the lifecycle:

| Action | Deadline | Responsible |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Identify vulnerabilities | Continuous | Manufacturer |
| Patch critical vulnerabilities | Without undue delay | Manufacturer |
| Inform users about patches | Together with update | Manufacturer |
| Report active exploitation | 24 hours to ENISA | Manufacturer |

### 3. Transparency and documentation

Each product must include:

- **Security manual**: secure configuration, known risks
- **Support policy**: duration of security updates
- **CE declaration of conformity**: verified compliance
- **Contact information**: vulnerability reporting point

### 4. Incident reporting

The CRA establishes a notification system:

1. **Immediate notification** (24h): to ENISA if there is active exploitation
2. **Incident notification** (72h): if it affects product security
3. **Final report** (14 days): analysis and measures taken

## Implications for law firms

### 1. Advising manufacturing clients

Firms must be prepared to:

- Assess whether a client's products fall within the scope of the CRA
- Classify products according to risk level (Class I or II)
- Review development contracts to include CRA obligations
- Advise on certification and CE declaration processes
- Manage incident notifications to ENISA

### 2. Contractual review

Software development contracts must be adapted:

```markdown
**Recommended clauses:**
- Security by design obligations
- Commitment to security patches for X years
- Vulnerability reporting procedure
- Liability in case of CRA non-compliance
- Right to security audit
```

### 3. Due diligence in M&A and financing

In corporate transactions, the CRA adds new due diligence elements:

- Inventory of target's digital products
- CRA compliance status by product
- History of vulnerabilities and patches
- Incident management processes
- Exposure to sanctions for non-compliance

## Sanctions and liability

### Sanctions regime

The CRA delegates to Member States the definition of sanctions, but establishes a minimum framework:

| Infringement | Indicative maximum sanction |
|--------------|----------------------------|
| Non-compliance with essential requirements | Up to €15M or 2.5% turnover |
| Non-compliance with incident reporting | Up to €10M or 1.5% turnover |
| Obstruction of authorities | Up to €5M or 1% turnover |

### Civil liability

The CRA interacts with the AI Liability Directive and the general civil liability regime:

- **Strict liability** for defective products (Product Liability Directive)
- **Liability for non-compliance** with CRA obligations
- **Collective actions** by mass affected parties in case of breaches

## CRA compliance checklist

### For manufacturers

**Design phase:**
- [ ] Documented cybersecurity risk assessment
- [ ] Approved secure architecture
- [ ] Security testing performed (pentesting)
- [ ] Complete technical documentation

**Commercialization phase:**
- [ ] CE declaration of conformity issued
- [ ] Security manual in user's language
- [ ] Published vulnerability contact point
- [ ] Defined update policy

**Post-sale phase:**
- [ ] Active vulnerability monitoring system
- [ ] Documented patching process
- [ ] ENISA reporting channel configured
- [ ] Incident register maintained

### For advising firms

- [ ] Team training on CRA requirements
- [ ] Adapted contract templates
- [ ] CRA due diligence checklist
- [ ] ENISA notification procedure
- [ ] Collaboration with technical experts established

## Comparison: CRA vs other regulations

| Aspect | CRA | NIS2 | DORA | GDPR |
|--------|-----|------|------|------|
| **Focus** | Digital products | Essential services | Financial sector | Personal data |
| **Obliged party** | Manufacturers | Important entities | Financial entities | Data controllers |
| **Cybersecurity** | By design | Risk management | Operational resilience | Data protection |
| **Reporting** | To ENISA | To national authority | To financial authority | To DPA |
| **Sanctions** | Up to €15M | Up to €10M | Up to 2% turnover | Up to €20M |

## The CRA and the legal tech ecosystem

### Software-developing firms

If your firm develops software for clients or for internal use that is marketed:

- **Assess the scope**: does it constitute a "digital product" under CRA?
- **Review your stack**: do you use third-party components? Verify their compliance
- **Document everything**: maintain a record of security design decisions

### Legal tech providers

Companies that sell software to law firms must:

- Obtain CE certification/declaration for their products
- Provide security manuals
- Establish vulnerability reporting channels
- Guarantee security updates throughout the lifecycle

## Conclusion

The Cyber Resilience Act represents the largest digital product cybersecurity regulation in EU history. For law firms, mastering the CRA is not optional: it is a necessary competency to advise clients in the technology sector and an obligation if they develop proprietary software.

The key lies in **early preparation**: understanding the scope, correctly classifying products, establishing vulnerability management processes, and adapting contracts and commercial practices.
