## The Question That Paralyzes Every Company

You have the idea clear. The team is ready. The budget, approved. But then comes the doubt that freezes more projects than any other:

**"Do we launch now with the basics or wait until we have everything?"**

It's the crossroads between the **MVP** (Minimum Viable Product) and the **full product**. And choosing wrong can cost you months of development, thousands of euros, and —worst of all— the market opportunity.

At LegaSint we've seen startups burn 18 months perfecting features nobody used. And traditional companies launch mediocre products that damaged their reputation. The key isn't in "more" or "less". It's in **knowing what to include at each moment**.

---

## What Is an MVP Really?

An MVP is not a broken product. It's not "what we had time to build." It's not a cheap version of what we want to construct.

> **An MVP is the minimum version of your product that solves your users' main problem and generates real value.**

It has to be **viable**, not incomplete. It has to work, not be a prototype. The difference is in scope, not quality.

### The MVP Test

If your product meets these three conditions, it's a valid MVP:

1. **Solves a concrete problem** for a specific group of users
2. **Works without critical errors** in the main flow
3. **Allows you to learn** if the solution works before investing more

If any are missing, it's not an MVP. It's a poorly designed experiment or an incomplete product.

## MVP vs Full Product: The Real Comparison

| Aspect | MVP | Full Product |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Validate that the solution works and there's demand | Serve all users and use cases |
| **Features** | Core + 1-2 key differentiators | Core + edge cases + integrations + customization |
| **Target users** | Early adopters tolerant of imperfections | Demanding end users |
| **Development time** | 6-12 weeks | 6-18 months |
| **Estimated budget** | €15,000 - €50,000 | €100,000 - €500,000+ |
| **Main risk** | Launching something that doesn't interest anyone | Investing a lot in something nobody wants |
| **Ideal moment** | Validated idea, uncertain market | Successful MVP, confirmed demand |

## When to Launch an MVP (and When Not To)

### ✅ Launch an MVP If:

- **Your idea isn't validated with real users.** You need evidence that someone will pay for this.
- **The market is new or uncertain.** Better to test with little before betting big.
- **You have fast competition.** Arriving first with the basics is worth more than arriving late with perfection.
- **Your budget is limited.** An MVP gives you real data to seek investment later.
- **You don't know which features will be used most.** Better let users tell you with their behavior.

### ❌ Don't Launch an MVP If:

- **Your product handles sensitive or regulated data.** (Health, banking, legal). Here quality and security are non-negotiable.
- **Your brand depends on the perception of excellence.** A mediocre launch can damage reputations built over years.
- **Users won't tolerate imperfections.** If your target is Fortune 500 companies, an MVP with bugs isn't an option.
- **You already have confirmed demand and waiting users.** If you have 500 paid reservations, maybe it's worth waiting for the full product.

## What to Include in an MVP: The 3-Question Framework

To decide what goes into your MVP, use this filter for each proposed feature:

### Question 1: Without This, Does the Product Solve the Main Problem?

If the answer is **no**, it's must-have. Include it.

If the answer is **yes**, move to question 2.

### Question 2: Would More Than 80% of Users Use It in the First 2 Weeks?

If the answer is **yes**, consider including it.

If the answer is **no**, postpone it.

### Question 3: Does the Implementation Cost Exceed the Value It Provides?

If implementing a feature costs 2 weeks but only 5% of users will use it... it's a hard no.

### Practical Example: MVP of a Task Management App

| Feature | Solves the problem? | 80% would use? | Worth the cost? | Include in MVP? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Create tasks | Yes | Yes | Yes | ✅ Yes |
| Assign tasks to team | Yes | Yes | Yes | ✅ Yes |
| Email notifications | No (nice-to-have) | Yes | Yes | ⚠️ Consider |
| Slack integration | No | 30% | 2 weeks | ❌ No |
| Advanced reports | No | 10% | 3 weeks | ❌ No |
| Native mobile app | No | 60% | 8 weeks | ❌ No |
| Dark mode | No | 40% | 2 days | ❌ No (post-MVP) |

**Resulting MVP:** Create tasks, assign them, mark as completed. Basic notifications if budget allows. Everything else, phase 2.

## The Most Expensive Mistake: The "Bloated MVP"

The worst enemy of an MVP isn't lack of features. It's **scope creep**: the unstoppable tendency to add "just one more thing."

> "If we're doing login, let's add social login too."
> "And if we add social login, also 2FA."
> "And if we add 2FA, also account recovery by SMS."
> "And while we're at it, user roles with granular permissions..."

**6 months later:** Your "MVP" has 47 features, none work quite right, and you still haven't launched.

### The "No by Default" Rule

Every feature proposed for the MVP starts with a **no**. Whoever proposes it has to prove why it's essential. Not the other way around.

## From MVP to Full Product: The Smart Roadmap

A successful MVP isn't the end. It's the **starting point**. But the transition requires strategy, not just "keep adding things."

### Phase 1: Metrics (Weeks 1-4 post-launch)

Before building more, observe:

- **Adoption:** How many sign up? How many come back?
- **Engagement:** What do they do in the app? Where do they drop off?
- **Retention:** Do they return on day 1? Day 7? Day 30?
- **Feedback:** What do they ask for? What frustrates them?

### Phase 2: Data-Driven Prioritization (Weeks 5-8)

With real data, prioritize the next block of features:

1. **Quick wins:** Features many users request and cost little to implement
2. **Blockers:** Things preventing key users from adopting the product
3. **Differentiators:** What sets you apart from competition

### Phase 3: Iteration (Month 3 onwards)

Continuous cycle of:

- Measure → Learn → Build → Measure

Each iteration adds real value, not features for the sake of adding features.

## Real Case: How an MVP Saved a Project (and How Another Nearly Sank It)

### Success: The Startup That Listened

A client of ours had an idea for a booking app for beauty professionals. Their original plan: native iOS + Android app, integrated payments, synchronized calendar, push notifications, admin panel, client app...

**Estimated budget:** €180,000. **Time:** 9 months.

We proposed an MVP: responsive web app with basic bookings, simple calendar, and email notifications. **€25,000. 8 weeks.**

They launched. 200 professionals registered in the first month. They discovered that what professionals valued most wasn't the mobile app, but automatic WhatsApp reminders (which they hadn't planned).

**They iterated.** Added WhatsApp. Grew to 2,000 professionals. Then, and only then, invested in native apps.

**Result:** They reached market 7 months earlier. Spent 6 times less in the first phase. Built what users actually wanted.

### Failure: The Company That Didn't Listen

Another client, a traditional logistics company, wanted to "digitize their operations." They invested €300,000 in a complete system: real-time tracking, integration with 12 carriers, driver app, executive dashboard, AI delivery prediction...

**18 months of development.** Launched with fanfare.

**Real users in the first month:** 12. Of the company's 200 drivers, 12 used the app. The rest continued with their manual processes because the system was slower than their previous method.

**The problem:** They never asked drivers what they needed. They built a Ferrari for people who needed a bicycle.

## Checklist: Is Your MVP Ready to Launch?

Before pressing the red button, verify:

- [ ] **The main flow works without critical errors.** (Sign up → Main action → Result)
- [ ] **Users can complete the main task without help.** No manual, no tutorial, no support.
- [ ] **The experience is coherent.** No half-designed screens or buttons that do nothing.
- [ ] **You have metrics configured.** You know what to measure from day 1.
- [ ] **You have a feedback channel.** Users can tell you what works and what doesn't.
- [ ] **You have an iteration plan.** It's not "launch and see what happens." It's "launch, measure, and improve."

---

## Conclusion

An MVP is not an excuse to do things poorly. It's a **strategy to learn fast without ruining yourself**.

The question isn't "MVP or full product?" The question is: **"What do I need to build to learn the most important thing as soon as possible?"**

Sometimes the answer is an MVP. Sometimes it's a full product. The important thing is that the decision is based on your situation, your market, and your validation —not on startup trends or fear of launching.
