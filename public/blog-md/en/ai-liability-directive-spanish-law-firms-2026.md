# AI Liability Directive: Practical Guide for Spanish Law Firms in 2026

Analysis of the new AI Liability Directive applicable from 2026: obligations, legal risks, and compliance strategies for law firms.

## Introduction

The **Directive on Liability for Damages Caused by Artificial Intelligence Systems** (AI Liability Directive) has become one of the most important regulatory pillars for the European digital ecosystem in 2026. Approved by the European Parliament and Council as an essential complement to the AI Act, this directive redefines who bears responsibility when an AI system causes harm.

For **Spanish law firms**, the AI Liability Directive is not just another regulation to advise clients on: it is a transformation of the civil liability framework that directly affects how contracts, insurance policies, and litigation strategies are structured. In this article, we analyze the practical implications of this directive and how firms can position themselves as leaders in this new landscape.

## 1. What is the AI Liability Directive?

### Background and Objective

The AI Liability Directive (2024/1689) was designed to harmonize civil liability rules across the European Union when it comes to damages caused by AI systems. Its main objective is to facilitate victims obtaining compensation when an artificial intelligence system causes them harm.

### Relationship with the AI Act

While the **AI Act** regulates the AI market (which systems can be marketed and under what conditions), the **AI Liability Directive** addresses the consequences when things go wrong. They are two sides of the same regulatory coin:

| Regulation | Focus | Scope |
|------------|-------|-------|
| **AI Act** | Prevention and pre-market compliance | Risk classification, technical requirements |
| **AI Liability Directive** | Post-damage remediation | Civil liability, evidence, compensation |

### Entry into Force

The directive was published in the Official Journal of the EU in October 2024, and Member States were required to transpose it into national law by **end of 2026**. Spain is currently in the process of legislative adaptation through amendments to the Civil Code and the Civil Procedure Law.

## 2. Fundamental Changes in Liability

### Burden of Proof Reversal

The most significant change introduced by the directive is the **presumption of causality**. Previously, if an AI system caused damage, the victim had to prove that the system's defect was the direct cause of the harm. This was practically impossible given the opacity of many algorithms.

Under the new directive:

- If the victim proves that the AI system violated the AI Act (for example, failed to meet transparency or safety requirements)
- And proves that the damage is consistent with the violation
- **It is presumed** that the system caused the damage
- It is the **developer or deployer** who must prove that there was no causation

### Scope of Application

The directive covers:

- **Material damage**: direct economic losses
- **Personal injury**: physical or psychological harm
- **Property damage**: including data and digital assets
- **Algorithmic discrimination**: when an AI system makes discriminatory decisions

### Available Defenses

AI providers can be exempt from liability by demonstrating:

1. That the damage was due to **force majeure** or a third party's act
2. That the victim modified the system without authorization
3. That they complied with all AI Act requirements and the damage was **unforeseeable**

## 3. Impact on Spanish Law Firms

### New Advisory Areas

The AI Liability Directive opens significant business opportunities for firms that specialize:

#### A. AI Liability Litigation

Litigation practices will see an increase in cases related to:
- Personnel selection systems that discriminate
- Autonomous vehicles with failures
- Medical diagnostic systems with errors
- Credit scoring algorithms that cause reputational damage

#### B. Contractual Review

AI development and licensing contracts will need specific clauses regarding:
- **Indemnifications** for AI Act non-compliance
- **Technology professional liability insurance**
- **SLAs** (Service Level Agreements) with penalties
- **Algorithmic compliance audits**

#### C. Preventive Compliance

Advising corporate clients on:
- Technical documentation required by the AI Act
- Risk and mitigation registers
- Transparency and explainability protocols
- Incident response plans

### Risks for the Firm Itself

Firms that use AI tools in their practice are also subject to the directive:

- **Legal research tools**: if an algorithm provides incorrect information that causes client harm
- **Outcome prediction systems**: if predictions are negligent
- **Client service chatbots**: if they provide incorrect legal advice

> **Recommendation**: Any firm using AI should review its professional liability insurance policies to ensure they cover algorithmic risks.

## 4. Compliance Strategies for Clients

### Step 1: AI Systems Audit

```markdown
- [ ] Inventory all AI systems used
- [ ] Classify according to AI Act (prohibited, high-risk, limited, minimal)
- [ ] Evaluate technical compliance
- [ ] Identify regulatory gaps
```

### Step 2: Regulatory Documentation

The directive presumes liability if adequate documentation does not exist. It is essential to maintain:

- **Design and development records** of the system
- **Fundamental rights impact assessments**
- **Validation and testing records**
- **Operations audit logs**
- **Incident and response records**

### Step 3: Insurance Coverage Review

Traditional professional liability policies do not cover algorithmic damages. It is necessary to:

- Negotiate **specific endorsements** for AI
- Consider **parametric insurance** based on algorithmic metrics
- Evaluate **captive insurance** for large AI deployers

### Step 4: Contractual Clauses

Example of updated indemnification clause:

> "The Provider guarantees that the AI System complies with Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act). In case of non-compliance, the Provider shall indemnify the Client for all direct and indirect damages, including litigation costs, administrative penalties, and reputational damages."

## 6. Practical Scenarios

### Case A: Firm Advises a Fintech

**Situation**: Fintech uses a credit scoring algorithm classified as "high-risk" under the AI Act.

**Firm's actions**:
1. Audit AI Act compliance (transparency, training data, biases)
2. Review insurance policy for algorithmic coverage
3. Draft indemnification clauses in contracts with the AI provider
4. Establish response protocol for claims

### Case B: Litigation for Algorithmic Discrimination

**Situation**: Candidate rejected by an AI personnel selection system.

**Firm's strategy**:
1. Request **access to the algorithm** (right to explanation)
2. Demonstrate **presumption of causality** (AI Act violation)
3. Demand **technical documentation** from the employer
4. Quantify damages (economic + moral damages)
