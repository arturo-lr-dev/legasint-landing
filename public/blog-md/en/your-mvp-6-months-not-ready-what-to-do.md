# Your MVP Is 6 Months In and Not Ready: What to Do

Has your MVP become an endless project? Discover the decision framework to know whether to kill it, pivot, or rescue it before it consumes your business.

## The case that hurts

María took her idea to a development agency in January. "I'll have my MVP in 3 months," she told her investors. August has arrived and all she has is a repository full of code that "almost works," a €45,000 invoice, and a technical team that every week discovers "one more thing that needs fixing before launch."

It's not an isolated case. It's the norm.

90% of MVPs get delayed. 42% of startups that outsource development end up changing providers before launch. And the real cost of an MVP that stretches to 6 months isn't just the €45,000 billed: it's the customers who never arrived, the competition that did launch, and the mental toll on a founding team that starts doubting its own idea.

This article isn't to tell you "be more patient." It's to give you a **decision framework** that lets you act with clarity: kill, pivot, or rescue.

---

## Why MVPs become endless projects

Before deciding what to do, understand what's happening. Delays usually aren't caused by bad faith. They're caused by a predictable combination of factors:

### 1. The "one more thing" syndrome

Every week a new feature emerges that "is critical for launch." An MVP is not a complete product. It's the minimum version that validates your main hypothesis. If you keep adding, you're not building an MVP: you're building a product without budget or validation.

### 2. The perfectionism trap

Your technical team wants to do it right. That's good. But sometimes "right" means scalable architectures for 100,000 users when you don't even have 100 yet. The best code is the one that lets you validate your idea this week, not the one that will handle millions of users in 2027.

### 3. Lack of a clear "done" definition

"When it works" is not a definition. "When we have payments" isn't either if you haven't defined what type of payments, with which gateway, and what exact flow. Ambiguity in requirements is the fuel of delays.

### 4. Accumulated technical debt

Decisions made in week 2 to "move fast" become obstacles in week 12. The code that "we'll fix later" never gets fixed. Every new feature is slower than the previous one.

### 5. The real problem: you don't know if your idea works

Here's the uncomfortable truth. If your MVP has stretched to 6 months, it's probably not just an execution problem. It's that **you haven't validated whether people want what you're building**. And the more you invest in building without validating, the harder it is to admit that maybe you should change direction.

## The framework: Kill, Pivot, or Rescue

Three options. None are easy. All are better than staying in inertia.

### Option A: Kill (End the project)

**When to consider it:**
- You've spent more than 70% of your initial budget and have nothing functional
- Your main hypothesis has changed 3 or more times during development
- Nobody outside your team has shown real interest (pre-sales, waitlist, positive interviews)
- The cost to finish exceeds the value it would provide, even if it works

**How to do it without ruining yourself:**
1. Do an honest inventory of what's been built. Is anything reusable?
2. Document the lessons. What failed, why, what you'd do differently.
3. Communicate it well. To investors, team, potential customers. Transparency generates respect.
4. Don't call it "failure." It's an expensive experiment that gave you valuable data.

> "I have not failed. I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work." — Thomas Edison (attributed)

### Option B: Pivot (Change direction)

**When to consider it:**
- There's market interest, but not for the exact solution you're building
- You've discovered a more urgent problem for your users while building
- The technology you chose is a bottleneck, but the idea is solid
- You have partial traction (some interested users, but not enough)

**Types of pivots that work:**
- **Problem pivot:** Same market, different problem. "They don't want task management, they want automatic invoicing."
- **Solution pivot:** Same problem, different solution. "They don't want an app, they want a WhatsApp bot."
- **Segment pivot:** Same solution, different market. "SMEs don't buy, freelancers do."
- **Technology pivot:** Same idea, different stack. "React Native is killing us, let's go web first."

**How to execute it:**
1. Define in one sentence what you've learned about your user.
2. Validate the new direction in 2 weeks maximum (landing page + interviews, not code).
3. Reuse everything you can from the previous project.
4. Set a realistic budget and timeline for the new MVP.

### Option C: Rescue (Save the project)

**When to consider it:**
- You're at 70-80% of something functional and the problem is execution, not idea
- You have users waiting or you've validated demand clearly
- The delay is due to accumulated technical debt, not lack of direction
- You have resources (time or money) for one last defined push

**The 4-week rescue plan:**

**Week 1: Brutal diagnosis**
- Technical audit: What works? What's critical technical debt? What's "nice to have"?
- Define the minimum "done": maximum 3 core features. Everything else goes to phase 2.
- Set a hard deadline: if it's not ready in 3 weeks, kill or pivot.

**Week 2: Major surgery**
- Eliminate everything that's not core. Yes, it hurts. Yes, it's necessary.
- Fix only the technical debt that blocks launch.
- Freeze new features. Zero exceptions.

**Week 3: Integration and testing**
- Connect the remaining pieces.
- Test with real users, not internally. 5 users will tell you more than 50 automated tests.
- Document critical bugs vs. cosmetic bugs. Only fix the critical ones.

**Week 4: Controlled launch**
- Soft launch: 10-20 users, no mass marketing.
- Collect metrics: Do they stay? Do they use the core feature? Are there critical errors?
- Decide: scale, iterate, or pivot based on real data.

## The question that matters

Before choosing Kill, Pivot, or Rescue, answer this with total honesty:

> **"If I launched tomorrow exactly what I have, would anyone use it and pay for it?"**

If the answer is "I'm not sure," your problem isn't the code. It's validation.

If the answer is "yes, but it's not ready," your problem is execution. Apply Rescue.

If the answer is "no, because I built something nobody asked for," your problem is direction. Consider Pivot or Kill.

---

## Lessons for your next MVP

Whatever your decision, take these rules to your next project:

1. **Define "done" before starting.** Write exactly what features the MVP includes and which it doesn't. Share it with the whole team.

2. **Budget double the time and money.** MVPs always cost more. If you think it's 3 months, budget 6. If you think it's €20,000, have €40,000 available.

3. **Validate before building.** A landing page + interest form + 20 interviews cost less than one week of development and give you more information.

4. **Launch before it's ready.** An MVP that slightly embarrasses its creator is the sweet spot. If it doesn't embarrass you, you've taken too long.

5. **Set decision points.** Every month, evaluate: are we on track? Is the hypothesis still valid? Should we continue, pivot, or stop?

## Conclusion

An MVP that stretches to 6 months isn't an anomaly. It's a signal. The signal that something in your process, your idea, or your execution needs to change.

The good news: having clarity about what's happening gives you the power to act. Kill, Pivot, or Rescue aren't endings. They're strategic decisions that get you closer to building something that truly matters.

The worst mistake isn't launching late. It's continuing to build something nobody wants, faster and faster.
